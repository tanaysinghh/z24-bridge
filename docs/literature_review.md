# Literature Review

## 1. WaveNet

WaveNet (van den Oord et al., 2016, *WaveNet: A Generative Model for Raw Audio*, arXiv:1609.03499) was introduced as an autoregressive generator of raw audio. Its building block, the **dilated causal convolution**, has since become a standard sequence encoder. A causal convolution only sees current and past samples. Dilation skips inputs at a fixed step that doubles from layer to layer (1, 2, 4, ..., 512), so the receptive field grows exponentially with depth while the parameter count grows only linearly. Each residual block applies a **gated activation**, `tanh(W_f * x) ⊙ σ(W_g * x)`, which lets the network decide per channel and time step how much filtered signal to pass on. **Residual connections** carry the block input forward, and **skip connections** from every block are summed into the output head, which keeps deep stacks trainable. For classification, the generative softmax over audio samples is replaced by global pooling over time and a linear class head. The network then works as a multi-resolution filter bank over the raw vibration signal: early layers resolve fast oscillations and deeper, highly dilated layers integrate behaviour over seconds. In this project the WaveNet uses 9 gated blocks (kernel 2, dilations 1-256, 64 residual/skip channels), giving a 512-sample (5.12 s) receptive field and about 229k parameters.

## 2. MiniRocket

MiniRocket (Dempster, Schmidt and Webb, 2021, *MINIROCKET: A Very Fast (Almost) Deterministic Transform for Time Series Classification*, KDD '21) simplifies ROCKET (Dempster, Petitjean and Webb, 2020, *Data Mining and Knowledge Discovery* 34). Both turn a time series into a large feature vector using **random convolutional kernels that are never trained**, then fit a linear classifier on top. MiniRocket fixes the kernel length to 9 and restricts weights to two values (-1 and 2, with three weights set to 2), giving 84 base kernels. These are applied at a set of **dilations** spread exponentially up to the input length, so the transform captures patterns at many time scales. Each kernel/dilation pair gets several **biases** drawn from quantiles of its convolution output on random training examples. Every feature is the **proportion of positive values (PPV)** of the biased convolution output. With the default of about 10,000 features (9,996 in practice), the transform is almost deterministic and very fast. Classification uses a **ridge regression classifier** on standardised features, with the regularisation strength chosen on held-out data. MiniRocket matches the accuracy of far more expensive deep ensembles on the UCR archive at a small fraction of the training cost. That makes it a strong baseline for small datasets such as single-channel Z24, where deep models overfit easily.

## 3. InceptionTime

InceptionTime (Ismail Fawaz et al., 2020, *InceptionTime: Finding AlexNet for Time Series Classification*, *Data Mining and Knowledge Discovery* 34) brings the Inception idea from computer vision to time series. Each **Inception module** first applies a 1x1 **bottleneck** convolution to reduce channel width. It then runs **parallel convolutions with kernel sizes 10, 20 and 40** next to a **max-pooling branch** followed by a 1x1 convolution. The four outputs are concatenated, batch-normalised and passed through a ReLU. Short kernels respond to fine, high-frequency structure and long kernels to slower patterns, so every module reads the signal at several scales at once. The reference network stacks six modules, adds a **residual shortcut every three modules**, and ends with global average pooling and a softmax layer. The original paper averages an ensemble of five such networks to reduce variance; this project trains a single network so that the comparison with WaveNet and MiniRocket is like-for-like. With 32 filters per branch and a 38-channel bottleneck, the model has about 494k parameters.

## 4. Prior work on the Z24 bridge

The Z24 bridge was a 60 m post-tensioned concrete box-girder bridge near Bern, Switzerland. In 1997-1998, under the EU SIMCES project, it became the reference benchmark for vibration-based structural health monitoring. It produced two datasets:

- **Long-term continuous monitoring** (Nov 1997 - Sep 1998). Peeters and De Roeck (2001, *Earthquake Engineering and Structural Dynamics* 30) showed that natural frequencies change by several percent with temperature, especially below freezing when the asphalt stiffens. These **environmental effects** can mask or mimic damage, and much later Z24 work focuses on separating environmental from structural variation.
- **Progressive Damage Tests (PDT)** (Aug-Sep 1998). The source of this project's data: 17 scenarios ranging from a reference state, through pier settlement of 20-95 mm, foundation tilt, concrete spalling, a landslide, concrete-hinge failure and anchor-head failure, to rupture of up to 6 of 16 post-tensioning tendons. Each scenario was measured under ambient excitation in 9 setups with a few fixed reference accelerometers and many roving ones (Krämer, De Smet and De Roeck, 1999, IMAC XVII; Maeck and De Roeck, 2003, *Mechanical Systems and Signal Processing* 17; Reynders and De Roeck, 2009, *Encyclopedia of Structural Health Monitoring*).

Classical PDT work uses **operational modal analysis**: identify natural frequencies, damping and mode shapes, then detect and locate damage from changes in modal parameters, modal curvature or flexibility. The pier settlement scenarios, for example, lower the first vertical-bending frequency from about 3.9 Hz to about 3.7 Hz. Data-driven methods (autoencoders, PCA and novelty detection, and more recently 1D CNNs and time-series classifiers) have since been applied directly to the Z24 acceleration records, mostly for binary damage detection or a few damage states. Multi-class identification of all 17 PDT scenarios from raw acceleration is much harder and less studied.

**Project baseline.** The baseline this capstone extends identified damage scenarios with **WaveNet and MiniRocket** on single-channel Z24 acceleration data and reported **60-65 % accuracy**. *[Citation and exact evaluation protocol to be added by the author.]* Comparisons should note the evaluation protocol. As `docs/dataset.md` and `docs/results_review2.md` explain, the records of one setup are contiguous slices of one continuous measurement. Random window-level splits therefore put near-duplicate signal in both train and test and inflate accuracy. This project uses a **setup-held-out split**, which is stricter, so its numbers are not directly comparable with random-split figures.
